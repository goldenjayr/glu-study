The Division of the Kingdom

931 BC to the return — the split, the sin of Jeroboam, and hope after exile.

Big ideas

What to teach and remember

  • The kingdom splits in 931 BC. Ahijah’s word names Jeroboam over ten tribes; Rehoboam takes the young men’s tax advice at Shechem; Shemaiah stops a civil war. God can tear a throne in a single assembly.
  • Jeroboam’s “pastoral” solution is calves at Bethel and Dan, a homemade feast, and priests who are not Levites. That counterfeit worship is called the sin of Jeroboam — and it lasts until Samaria falls in 722 BC.
  • Judah is not innocent. Rehoboam leads apostasy; Shishak of Egypt strips the temple. A southern address does not make a faithful king.
  • Asa tastes revival, then fails. A good start is not the same as a guarded heart. Preach the whole reign, not the highlight reel.
  • The north runs Nadab, Baasha, Elah, Zimri, Omri (who builds Samaria), then Ahab and Jezebel — with Elijah standing in the way. Thrones change; the calves stay.
  • Captivity has four reasons. Assyria and Babylon are the rod; the Lord is the One who swung it. Judgment is covenant love that will not share a people with other gods.
  • Hope after the night: Cyrus sends them home; Zerubbabel raises the Second Temple by 515 BC. After restoration they cling to Torah, gather in synagogue, and (at last) put away the idols.

Quiz drill
Quiz drill / review · open to practice · 10 items

Quiz score: 100% · 1 attempt

1. Jeroboam was humanly responsible for splitting the Kingdom.

TrueAhijah named him, and Rehoboam’s folly at Shechem opened the door — Jeroboam took the ten tribes. God split it; a man still bears the human share.

2. Ahab and Jezebel were known as two of Israel's godliest leaders.

FalseThey are the opposite — Baal, Naboth’s vineyard, and a throne that hunted prophets. Elijah stands against them, not with them.

3. Rehoboam was a great leader of revival.

FalseRevival is Asa’s word, not Rehoboam’s. He took the young men’s tax advice, then led Judah into apostasy until Shishak came.

4. Jehoshaphat was known for his wise choice of allies.

FalseHe is remembered for joining himself to Ahab. Unequal alliance is not wisdom; it nearly cost him the kingdom.

5. Uzziah's reign was one of the briefest in Judah's history.

FalseUzziah (Azariah) reigned fifty-two years — one of the longest, not the briefest. Pride in the temple is the warning, not a short calendar.

6. Josiah was known for his rampant immorality.

FalseJosiah is the boy-king of the book — he tore down the high places. Immorality is not his headline; repentance is.

7. Daniel lived most of his life in Babylon.

TrueTaken young, he stayed through empires. Faithfulness is not always a ticket home; sometimes it is a long obedience in a foreign court.

8. The captives returned to Jerusalem during the reign of Persia's King Cyrus.

TrueIsaiah named him; Ezra records the decree. The Lord stirs a pagan king so a remnant can go home.

9. The builder of the Second Temple was Zerubbabel.

TrueZerubbabel laid the house that stood until Herod and until Christ walked its courts — finished about 515 BC.

10. Ahab's closest advisor was Elijah.

FalseElijah was Ahab’s antagonist, not his counselor. Jezebel is the voice in that palace; the prophet is the voice against it.
Essay themes

If you preach or review

Four reasons for Captivity

The exile is not an accident of empire. This lesson names four reasons the people went into captivity: they would not quit idolatry (the calves and the Baals); they would not keep the covenant word; they would not hear the prophets God sent; and they would not give the land its Sabbath rest. Assyria and Babylon are the rod — the Lord is the One who swung it. Preach captivity as holy love that will not let a people keep two gods.

Jeroboam's apostasy

Fear dressed as policy: if they go up to Jerusalem, they may return to Rehoboam. So Jeroboam sets calves at Bethel and Dan, invents a feast, and ordains priests who are not Levites. Convenient worship is still rebellion. Kings keep changing; this sin keeps its name until Samaria falls in 722 BC. A church can copy it — a more local god, a more flexible calendar, and leaders we chose ourselves.

Israel after Restoration

Cyrus sends them home. Zerubbabel raises the Second Temple by 515 BC. After the return they cling to Torah, gather in synagogue, and — at last — put away the idols that had cost them the land. Exile taught what sermons had not. Hope is not a new empire; it is a remnant, a book, and a house of prayer until Messiah comes.
