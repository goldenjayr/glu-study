Prolegomena: Theologizing, Presuppositions, and Soteriology

Theology starts with Prolegomena — how we gather truth, test it, and name our presuppositions before we speak of salvation.

CompleteQuiz 100%
At a glance

Summary

Collecting data comes first in theologizing. The scientific method begins with a problem and is not identical to deduction. Everyone brings presuppositions; the Bible believer starts with God who has revealed Himself, while the liberal starts with mankind as pinnacle. Theology begins with Prolegomena. Salvation is not eschatology — it is both an instantaneous event and a progressive experience, studied as soteriology.

Essay themes

If you preach or review

Five tests used to verify theological truth

The five tests I would use are consistency, practicality, relevance, adequacy, and comprehensiveness. Consistency asks whether the claims fit together without contradiction. Practicality asks whether the truth corresponds to reality and speaks meaningfully to life. Relevance distinguishes what is essential from what is incidental. Adequacy recognizes that theology has a firm center in God's historical and supernatural revelation in Christ, while not pretending to draw a rigid boundary around every question. Comprehensiveness requires a careful examination of all questions related to the subject, so that nothing important is ignored.

Five presuppositions of the biblical believer

The five presuppositions of the biblical believer are: (1) There is a God, and He has revealed Himself in the Bible. (2) There are laws that are absolute. (3) People can know things to the degree that they directly observe them. (4) Truth is not relative; it is consistent with itself and corresponds to reality. (5) The mind accepts what is logical and rejects what is illogical.

Soteriology: definition, four aspects, and three tenses

Soteriology is the doctrine or study of salvation, from the Greek words soteria (salvation) and logos (word, study, or doctrine). Its four aspects are atonement—the historical work Christ accomplished on the cross; conversion—the human experience of turning from sin to faith; justification—the non-experiential, legal declaration of righteousness before God; and sanctification—the continuing growth into Christlikeness in the new life with Christ. Salvation has three tenses: past, we have been saved from the guilt and penalty of sin; present, we are being saved from the habit and dominion of sin; and future, we will be saved from bodily infirmity and the effects of sin at Christ's return.
