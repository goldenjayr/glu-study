Summary

Before the Second Great Awakening in the West, public morals were non-existent. James McGready catalyzed revival in Logan County, Kentucky; camp meetings introduced public emotion in worship. Baptists did not nearly disappear. Finney’s altar call was not welcomed by all denominations; his sermons often ran in next-day papers. Robert Raikes fathered the Sunday School movement. The Layman’s Prayer Revival was not birthed in Chicago (Fulton Street, NYC / Lamphier). Moody regretted giving no invitation the Sunday before the Chicago Fire; Ira Sankey was his music leader.

Essay themes

If you preach or review

Describe the Camp Meeting Awakenings (1780-1820)

The Camp Meeting Awakenings grew out of the moral and spiritual decline that followed the American Revolution. In Logan County, Kentucky, Presbyterian preacher James McGready called people to regular fasting and prayer, and the communion meetings he led became too large for church buildings. At Cane Ridge in 1801, an estimated 10,000–20,000 people gathered, with many arriving in wagons and staying for days. Preachers proclaimed repentance, salvation, heaven, and hell in the open air, and people responded with deep emotion—weeping, shouting, trembling, and coming to Christ. These gatherings became known as camp meetings. They combined fellowship, heartfelt singing, long sermons, and evangelism, and later became more organized. Baptists and Methodists embraced them, while circuit-riding preachers carried the gospel and planted churches across the frontier.

Describe the General Awakening (1830-1840), and the paradigm shifts and innovations that it introduced

The General Awakening of 1830–1840 was closely associated with Charles Grandison Finney. After his conversion, Finney left the law and preached revival, especially in Rochester. His “new measures” included the public invitation or altar call and the anxious seat, asking hearers to make an immediate, personal response to the gospel. Protracted meetings, lay participation, carrying revival results into nearby communities, organized evangelistic music, and the deliberate creation of a worship atmosphere also became important. The movement emphasized a practical Arminian appeal rather than relying on older Calvinistic assumptions. Print media helped spread the message, since Finney’s sermons were published in newspapers soon after preaching. These innovations connected worship and evangelism and helped revival spread widely.

Explain the inception and expansion of the Layman's Prayer Revival

The revival began during a time of economic growth and then the severe financial panic of 1857. At Fulton Street Church in New York City, missionary Jeremiah Lamphier organized a noon prayer meeting. Only a few attended at first, but the meeting grew rapidly as workers left their shops to pray. Newspapers reported the meetings, and prayer gatherings spread to other cities. Where factories were far from churches, owners provided tents. The movement was driven by ordinary laymen rather than a famous evangelist or a major crusade. Meetings were open and orderly but participatory: people prayed, exhorted, sang, or testified, usually within a five-minute limit. Conversions multiplied across the country, with prayer meetings in many cities and rural communities. The Civil War later disrupted the movement, but the revival showed how God used simple, faithful prayer by lay people.
