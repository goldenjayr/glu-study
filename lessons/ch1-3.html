How Shall We Divide Church History?

Dividing church history by the seven churches of Revelation as seven epochs — from Apostolic Ephesus through Liberal Laodicea — with prophecy as history pre-written, Reformation and missionary ages, and the end of history as the beginning of eternity.

At a glance

Summary

Lesson 3 asks how we should divide church history by reading the seven letters to the seven churches in Revelation as seven epochs. Prophecy is history pre-written. The seven letters do not follow a traveler’s path through Asia Minor. Ephesus represents the early Apostolic church; Smyrna the age of persecution (including attempts to destroy the Scriptures); Pergamos the state/political church; Thyatira the Medieval church (Christianity modified by medieval civilization; no vast Charlemagne revival); Sardis the Protestant/Reformation period (capitalizing on nationalism); Philadelphia the Missionary church; and Laodicea the Liberal age (liberalism does not free evangelistic zeal). The end of history is the beginning of eternity.

Big ideas

What to teach and remember

  • Prophecy is history pre-written.
  • The seven letters to the seven churches do NOT follow a traveler’s path visiting the churches in Asia Minor.
  • Ephesus = early Apostolic church (to ~100).
  • Smyrna = Persecuted church (100–312); a major tragedy was the attempt to destroy all copies of the Scriptures.
  • Pergamos = State/Political church (~316–800).
  • Thyatira = Medieval church (800–1517); Christianity was modified with medieval civilization; did NOT see a vast revival under Charlemagne.
  • Sardis = Protestant/Reformation (1517–1750); the Reformation capitalized on the spirit of nationalism.
  • Philadelphia = Missionary church (1750–1900).
  • Laodicea = Liberal church (1900+); liberalism does NOT free evangelistic zeal for unparalleled progress.
  • The end of history is the beginning of eternity.

Essay themes

If you preach or review

List the seven churches of Revelation and the historic epochs they represent.

The seven churches represent seven broad epochs of church history: Ephesus, the Apostolic Church (Pentecost–AD 100); Smyrna, the Persecuted Church (AD 100–312); Pergamos, the State or Political Church (about AD 316–800); Thyatira, the Medieval Church (AD 800–1517); Sardis, the Protestant or Reformation Church (AD 1517–1750); Philadelphia, the Missionary Church (AD 1750–1900); and Laodicea, the Liberal Church (AD 1900 onward).

From the perspective of Sardis, explain factors and personalities which contributed to the birth and development of the state church, the Period of Reformation.

From Sardis, the Reformation grew out of serious spiritual and moral problems in the medieval church, but also from several movements and personalities God used to prepare the way. The Albigenses and Waldensians, John Wycliffe, John Huss, and Jerome Savonarola challenged corruption and called people back to Scripture; the Renaissance, the printing press, nationalism, and widespread discontent also helped the reform spread. Wycliffe translated the New Testament into English, Huss was burned in 1415, and Savonarola was tortured and executed. Martin Luther then rediscovered that salvation rests on faith in God's grace rather than works, opposed Tetzel's indulgences, posted the Ninety-five Theses, and refused to recant. Zwingli led reform in Switzerland, Calvin's Institutes shaped Reformed theology, and John Knox established the Scottish Reformation. Together, these influences broke the monopoly of the papacy and helped form Protestant and national/state churches, with the Bible as the authority for faith and practice and with personal, living faith.

What characteristics marked the Missionary Church, depicted in the Church of Philadelphia?

Philadelphia was a faithful and persevering church with only limited strength, yet Christ gave it an open door for ministry. It kept His word and did not deny His name, so its defining marks were endurance, evangelistic zeal, revival, and worldwide missions. After about 1780, spiritual vitality spread through men and movements, especially John Wesley and the Methodist movement in Britain. Mission societies carried the gospel to every continent and many islands, while other religious groups and evangelists grew outside the established church. This period also showed the church's social influence in opposing the slave trade and improving living conditions and education for ordinary people. Philadelphia therefore represents a church that remained faithful and used its opportunity to proclaim Christ broadly.
