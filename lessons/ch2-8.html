Philadelphia: The Missionary Church 1750 – 1900 AD

Moravian Herrnhut prayer; Edwards and the First Great Awakening; Wesleys, Whitefield, open-air; Haystack missions; circuit riders; Cane Ridge.

At a glance

Summary

The Moravian community at Herrnhut (“the Lord’s watch”) began a prayer meeting lasting over a hundred years from August 13, 1727. John Newton wrote Amazing Grace. Jonathan Edwards issued a call to prayer and preached “Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God,” marking the beginning of the First Great Awakening in America. The Wesleys and Whitefield were in Oxford’s Holy Club; Wesley did not immediately embrace open-air preaching when Whitefield first invited him. The Haystack Prayer Meeting of 1806 helped birth modern U.S. missions. Methodist circuit riders typically covered 200–500 miles a month. Cane Ridge was not marked by somber, quiet dignity.

Essay themes

If you preach or review

Describe the Moravian Revival

The Moravian Revival began among persecuted believers who found refuge on Count Nicolaus von Zinzendorf’s estate at Herrnhut, Germany. After division and criticism, the community covenanted to serve the Lord and experienced a powerful visitation of God while gathering for the Lord’s Supper on August 13, 1727. They began a continuous twenty-four-hour prayer meeting that continued for one hundred years. From this prayer movement, the Moravians sent hundreds of missionaries around the world.

Describe the ministry of George Whitefield

George Whitefield was a gifted evangelist who preached repentance, holy living, and personal conversion through faith in Christ. When church pulpits closed to him, he preached outdoors, first to coal miners near Bristol, where crowds grew from about 200 to more than 20,000 and thousands were converted. In America he preached throughout the colonies in fields, orchards, barns, and other open places, often drawing thousands. His biblical, energetic preaching and use of open-air meetings helped spread the Great Awakening and encouraged the Methodist movement.

Describe the work of the Methodist Circuit Riders

Methodist Circuit Riders were itinerant preachers who traveled roughly 200 to 500 miles each month on horseback, serving thirty to fifty preaching points. They were often not college educated and came from ordinary trades, but they were trained and empowered by the Holy Spirit. They slept in homes, inns, or the open air, received little pay, preached passionately from the Bible without reading a manuscript, and used everyday illustrations. They organized converts into societies and churches, helping carry the gospel into remote communities and laying foundations for Methodist growth.
