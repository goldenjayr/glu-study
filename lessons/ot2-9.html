Minor Prophets

The Twelve — decline, hope, and the Redeemer.

At a glance

Summary

The Twelve — decline, hope, and the Redeemer. The Twelve belong together. God raised these prophets in days of decline and apostasy — not a golden age. When the nation is sliding, the Lord still sends a voice.

CompleteQuiz 100%
Big ideas

What to teach and remember

  • The Twelve belong together. God raised these prophets in days of decline and apostasy — not a golden age. When the nation is sliding, the Lord still sends a voice.
  • Five-fold emphases mark the books: the word is local, it addresses Israel, it names the Gentiles (often as tools of chastisement), it is predictive near and far, and it points to a promised Redeemer.
  • Jonah is the Lord’s sovereignty and a universal offer of salvation. Gentiles are not outside His mercy — the same God who uses the nations as a rod also calls them to live.
  • Amos is not a sophisticated businessman; he is a herdsman of Tekoa. Hosea and Gomer are not an ideal marriage. God picks the man, and sometimes the prophet’s home is the sermon.
  • Joel preaches the Day of the Lord and the outpouring of the Spirit. He and Elisha were contemporaries — the later writing prophets stand in the same stream as the earlier voices.
  • Micah 5:2 names Bethlehem as the Messiah’s birthplace. Keep that text close: the Savior’s town is not a Christmas decoration; it is predictive prophecy of the Redeemer.
  • Nahum explains God’s judgment on Nineveh, Assyria. Habakkuk 2:4 still stands. Zephaniah is of Hezekiah’s line — great-great grandson. Judgment and hope sit in one family tree.

Quiz drill
Quiz drill / review · open to practice · 10 items

Quiz score: 100% · 1 attempt

1. The Minor Prophets were raised up during times of decline and apostasy.

TrueThe Twelve are not a golden-age choir. God raises them when the nation is sliding — decline and apostasy are the soil, not the exception.

2. In the Minor Prophets, the Gentiles were often identified as tools of chastisement against Israel.

TrueAssyria, Babylon, and the nations around them are the rod. The Lord uses Gentiles to chasten His people — and still has a word for those nations too.

3. Predictive prophecy relates to a promised redeemer.

TrueNear and far, the predictive word is not a parlor trick. It relates to a promised Redeemer — hope with a name, not only a calendar.

4. In Jonah, we find a universal offer of salvation.

TrueNineveh hears and lives. Jonah is sovereignty plus a wide door: salvation is not Israel’s private stock.

5. Amos was known as a sophisticated businessman.

FalseAmos is a herdsman of Tekoa. God picks the man; the résumé does not have to look like the palace.

6. Hosea and Gomer were known as a couple with an ideal marriage.

FalseThe marriage is the sermon, not the postcard. Unfaithful Gomer, faithful Hosea — a living picture of Israel and the Lord.

7. Joel and Elisha were contemporaries.

TrueThe pack sets them in the same stretch of history. Joel’s Day of the Lord sits beside the earlier prophetic stream, not after it.

8. Micah's prophetic book predicts the birthplace of the Savior (Messiah).

TrueMicah 5:2 — Bethlehem. The Messiah’s town is named in the Twelve; Advent does not invent it.

9. Nahum was written to explain God's judgment on Ninevah, Assyria.

TrueThat is the book’s burden: Nineveh (Ninevah in the quiz) under the Lord’s sentence. Assyria is not too big for judgment.

10. Zephaniah was the great-great grandson of Hezekiah.

TrueHe stands in Hezekiah’s line — great-great grandson. Royal blood does not cancel a prophet’s call to preach judgment and hope.
Essay themes

If you preach or review

Five-fold emphases of the Minor Prophets

The Twelve preach with five emphases, not twelve unrelated hobbies. First, the word is local — it lands on the people standing in front of the prophet. Second, it addresses Israel: covenant people under a covenant God. Third, it names the Gentiles, often as tools of chastisement against Israel, and sometimes as hearers of mercy. Fourth, it is predictive, near and far — what is at the gate, and what is still over the horizon. Fifth, it relates to a promised Redeemer. For the pulpit: do not reduce the Minor Prophets to weather reports. Preach the local word, the nation’s sin, the nations as rod and audience, the near and far horizon, and Christ.

Major movements in Jonah

Jonah’s major movements are the Lord’s, not the fish’s. The prophet is sent; he runs; the Sovereign still has him. Then the word is preached, and a Gentile city lives — a universal offer of salvation in the mouth of a reluctant Israelite. The last movement is the argument over mercy: Jonah wants Nineveh judged; God has compassion and the last word. Sovereignty and a wide door sit in one short book. Preach it that way: the Lord overrules the flight, offers life to the nations, and will not let His servant shrink the gospel to one people.

Who Micah was / most important message (Bethlehem / Messiah)

Micah is one of The Twelve, raised in the same decline as the rest. His most important message is not a catalog of woes; it is the birthplace of the Savior. Micah 5:2 names Bethlehem as the Messiah’s town — predictive prophecy of the Redeemer, near enough to locate and far enough to wait. When you preach Advent or the incarnation, start here: the Minor Prophets already knew the village. Hope has an address. Hold Micah as a preacher of judgment who still points to the Child.
